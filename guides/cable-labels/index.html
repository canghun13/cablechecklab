Cable guide

How to identify USB-C cable power, data and video claims.

Read the power and data markings separately. If the packaging or rating is missing, keep it unknown: neither a USB-C plug nor a successful charge reveals the cable’s maximum capability.

Power markings answer one question

USB-IF’s current certified C-to-C cable power logos use 60 W or 240 W. More than 60 W requires a 5 A electronically marked cable. A legacy 100 W / 5 A claim can still describe an older compliant product, but it is not the current certified new-cable power logo.

Data labels should say the rate

Prefer explicit performance language—USB 5Gbps, 10Gbps, 20Gbps, 40Gbps, or 80Gbps—over generation names. USB 2.0 charge-first cables remain common and may be sufficient for charging while being unsuitable for fast storage or a full-featured display path.

ClaimWhat it establishesWhat it does not establish
240 WPower class, when genuine and applicableHigh-speed data or video

USB4 40GbpsDeclared USB4 link classExact host display count or dock behavior
ThunderboltCertified ecosystem capability for the marked generationEvery monitor mode on every host

Identify a cable without its packaging

  1. Inspect both connector housings and the cable jacket for watts, data rate, brand, and model. A color, thickness, or connector shape is not a capability record.
  2. Match the exact model and length to the manufacturer specification. If it claims USB-IF certification, check the USB-IF product search; a look-alike logo alone is not a verified record.
  3. Separate what is known: power rating, USB data class, and the intended display or USB4/Thunderbolt path. Do not fill an absent rating with a guessed wattage.
  4. Enter only those declarations in the Cable Capability Decoder. Leave power as Not stated / unknown if no rating is available; the result is a verification checklist, not a hardware test.

What an e-marker or tester can establish

A capable cable reader can retrieve advertised electronic identity fields, such as current capability, cable type, and supported signaling. That is different from testing the cable’s signal quality. USB-IF and Thunderbolt certification include electrical and interoperability checks in addition to identity data.

EvidenceUseful conclusionStill unknown
E-marker readingThe fields that this reader actually reportsPhysical condition, certified compliance, or success on every host
Power meterPower observed during that charging sessionThe cable’s maximum rating; charger, device and charge state also affect the reading
Transfer or display testThe tested mode works on that exact pathUntested higher rates, other modes, or which component limits a failed test

Failure to obtain a marker reading is not proof of a particular power or data class. Check the reader’s capabilities and setup. This website cannot query an e-marker, inspect the wiring, or detect a connected cable.

Video is a path, not a cable sticker

A full-featured cable can carry DisplayPort Alt Mode only when the source port emits video and the sink or adapter accepts the intended mode. Purpose-built USB-C-to-DisplayPort or USB-C-to-HDMI cables are directional adapters, not general C-to-C data cables.

Active versus passive is another separate claim

Active cables include electronics in the signal path. Do not treat “active” as automatically faster, bidirectional, or compatible with every USB, video, and Thunderbolt mode. Verify the exact supported protocols, direction, power requirements, and length in the product documentation.

Length requires exact-model evidence

Signal integrity depends on rate, construction, certification, active electronics, and exact length. That is why Cable Check Lab does not offer a universal cable-length calculator: public standards do not turn one length number into a safe compatibility verdict.

Choose a controlled next test

Use a known-capable host and endpoint with one direct cable. Keep the port, device and workload unchanged when swapping cables. A low result is evidence about the whole path, not proof of a bad cable. Use the Data Path Checker for declared rate bottlenecks or Charge Check for charging requirements. Do not push an unverified cable to a higher load to discover its rating; stop using damaged or overheating components.
